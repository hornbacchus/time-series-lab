## What It Does
GCC-PHAT (Generalized Cross-Correlation with Phase Transform) estimates the time delay between two signals using a frequency-domain method borrowed from acoustics and radar. It whitens the cross-spectrum — keeping only the phase information and discarding the magnitude — so that a single sharp peak marks the delay, even when the two signals have very different amplitude spectra. Its distinctive strength is *sub-sample* resolution: by interpolating the correlation it can resolve a delay to a fraction of a sampling interval.

## When to Use It
- You need a precise time delay between two signals that are broadly similar but offset in time.
- You want sub-sample delay resolution (finer than one observation interval).
- Your signals are broadband (rich across frequencies), as in acoustic, sensor, or high-frequency settings.
- Use GCC-PHAT for precise broadband delay estimation; use `cross_correlation_lag` for a general lead-lag with a significance test, or when the signals are narrowband (see the caveat below).

## How to Read the Result
The output is the estimated delay (in samples), a peak value, a signal-to-noise ratio, and a bootstrap confidence interval. A positive delay means the first series leads the second — the same convention as `cross_correlation_lag`, so the two agree in direction. On a broadband test signal where the first series leads the second by 5 samples, GCC-PHAT recovers the delay as exactly 5.0, with a high peak and a tight confidence interval. The sub-sample capability shows on non-integer delays: a true delay of 5.5 samples is recovered as 5.5 (on the Balanced and Thorough presets, whose interpolation resolves below one sample; the Fast preset works at integer resolution). The signal-to-noise ratio gauges confidence in the peak — a high value means a clean, unambiguous delay. The key prerequisite is in the method's name: the phase transform whitens the spectrum, which works beautifully for broadband signals but amplifies noise in the empty frequency bands of a narrowband or smooth series — so for narrowband data prefer the ordinary cross-correlation.

## Related Techniques
- *(alternatives)* `cross_correlation_lag` for a general lead-lag with a significance test and for narrowband signals; `prewhitened_ccf_lag` when autocorrelation within each series confounds the lag; `dtw_alignment_lag` when the offset varies over time rather than being a single constant delay.

## Technical Detail
The two series' cross-spectrum is computed by FFT and whitened by the phase transform (dividing by its magnitude, keeping only phase). The whitened cross-spectrum is then inverse-transformed to a length that is an integer multiple of the base resolution — this frequency-domain zero-padding genuinely interpolates the correlation, giving sub-sample resolution of one over the interpolation factor (set by preset: integer resolution on Fast, finer on Balanced and Thorough). The delay is the location of the correlation peak, signed so that a positive value means the first series leads the second (matching `cross_correlation_lag`). A bootstrap over the signal yields the confidence interval. Because the phase transform whitens all frequencies equally, the method requires a broadband signal — on a narrowband series the whitening amplifies empty-band noise and degrades the estimate.
*Reference run:* a broadband white-noise signal where the first series leads the second by 5 samples (n≈2048), Balanced — recovered delay 5.0 (exact across all presets), peak value about 0.99, signal-to-noise ratio about 32, 95% bootstrap confidence interval [5.0, 5.0]; a non-integer delay of 5.5 samples is recovered as 5.5 on Balanced and Thorough (sub-sample resolution).
